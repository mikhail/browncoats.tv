Prototype thesis

Measure the demand before renting the home.

Firefly has a fanbase that has already proven long-term loyalty. A low-risk demand test can quantify that signal — tier interest, RSVP intent, community opt-ins — before any infrastructure or rights commitment.

Why now

Use platforms for reach. Preserve first-party fan demand and leverage.

Platform distribution can reach viewers, but it often keeps the customer relationship, the merchandising path, the watch data, and the programming surface. A direct fan hub gives the Firefly team measurable demand data and negotiating leverage — whether the series ultimately lives on a platform, goes direct, or both.

30-day demand test

This prototype demonstrates a low-risk demand validation pilot. The official team could run it with their own vendor, OTT platform, or legal stack. Nothing here requires this specific site — the metrics and approach are what matter.

01

Waitlist / RSVP intent

How many fans express interest in early access or premiere events? Measures raw demand volume.

02

Membership tier interest

Which hypothetical tier (e.g. base access vs. premium) draws more interest? Reveals willingness-to-pay signal without collecting payments.

03

Community opt-ins

How many fans want ongoing community access (Discord, forums, newsletters)? Measures engagement depth beyond passive viewership.

04

Merch & extras interest

Do fans express interest in merchandise, behind-the-scenes content, or creator access? Tests ancillary revenue potential.

05

Geography & source attribution

Where do interested fans come from, and how did they find this? Informs regional licensing and marketing spend decisions.

06

Qualitative feedback

What do fans say they want most? Open-ended signal helps prioritize features, content cadence, and community format.

Platform economics

Use platforms for reach. Preserve first-party fan demand and leverage.

Every distribution channel involves trade-offs. Understanding the economics helps the Firefly team decide where to syndicate, where to go direct, and how to preserve the fan relationship.

YouTube

Watch-page ads offer roughly 55% creator revenue share. Memberships and fan funding net around 70%. But the platform owns the audience relationship and algorithm-driven discovery.

Good for reach and discovery. Less control over audience data and programming surface.

Amazon Prime Video Direct

Selected/licensed content at Amazon’s discretion. Prime payout rate-card can be as low as roughly $0.01–$0.12/hour in the US. TVOD splits around 50%. Payment lag can be significant.

Massive catalog reach. Revenue per-viewer and audience ownership are limited.

Netflix

Closed licensing/acquisition model. Not a self-serve platform — content is selected, not uploaded. No direct fan relationship or self-serve revenue channel.

Premium validation if selected. No direct fan demand signal or ongoing relationship.

Vimeo OTT / white-label

Direct-to-consumer platform economics: typically around $1/subscriber/month plus merchant fees. Useful as a white-label vendor route for running a branded fan hub.

One possible vendor for a Firefly-owned hub. Not the only option — the team would choose their own stack.

Precedent: Dropout

Direct-to-fan for creative freedom and reliable audience economics.

Dropout (formerly CollegeHumor) moved to a direct subscription model for creative independence and more predictable audience economics. Their experience shows that a dedicated fanbase can sustain a direct platform when the content and community are strong enough. Firefly’s proven multi-decade fandom suggests a similar opportunity — worth measuring before committing to a platform-first strategy.

Dropout’s context differs from Firefly’s. This is a directional precedent, not a 1:1 comparison.
